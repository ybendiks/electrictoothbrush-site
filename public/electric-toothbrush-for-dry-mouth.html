Why Dry Mouth Changes Brushing Needs

Dry mouth (xerostomia) — caused by medications, radiation therapy, Sjögren's syndrome, or other conditions — significantly increases decay risk because saliva's natural acid-buffering and remineralising action is reduced. People with dry mouth often have more plaque accumulation, faster acid attack, and greater tooth sensitivity. Brushing effectively becomes more important and more challenging: the friction of brushing on a less-lubricated surface can cause discomfort, and aggressive technique can accelerate enamel wear.

What to Look For

  • Sonic technology: Sonic brushes create fluid dynamics that clean slightly beyond the bristle tips — useful when saliva cannot help distribute cleaning action
  • Sensitive mode: Lower vibration speed reduces discomfort on dry, sensitive enamel
  • Extra-soft brush heads: Reduce friction on dry tissue
  • Pressure sensor: Prevents over-brushing, which accelerates enamel loss with dry mouth

Brushing Tips for Dry Mouth

  • Rinse with water before brushing to add moisture
  • Use a fluoride toothpaste (saliva normally helps distribute fluoride — use more time)
  • Brush at Level 1 / Sensitive mode to reduce friction
  • Replace brush heads every 2 months rather than 3 — they wear faster with reduced lubrication
  • Discuss prescription fluoride or remineralising products with your dentist

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sonic or oscillating better for dry mouth?

Sonic is generally preferred for dry mouth because the fluid dynamics created by high-speed vibration supplement the reduced saliva flow. Oscillating-rotating brushes rely more on direct bristle contact and are slightly more dependent on adequate lubrication.

Can electric toothbrushes help with dry mouth?

They help you brush more effectively despite dry mouth by reducing the required manual technique. They do not treat dry mouth itself — speak to your doctor or dentist about treating the underlying cause and managing symptoms with saliva substitutes.
